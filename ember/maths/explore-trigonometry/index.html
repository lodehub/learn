The puzzle: measure something you can't reach

There is a tall tree in the park. You want its height. You can't climb it, you have no drone, and the tape measure is only 5 metres long.

People had exactly this problem for thousands of years, with things much bigger than trees: the height of a pyramid, the width of a river, the distance to the Moon. Trigonometry is what they built to solve it.

Same shape, any size

Here's a right-angled triangle. You control the angle at the bottom-left and the size. The side lengths are what a ruler would measure.

Predict first

Keep the angle the same and make the triangle bigger. What happens to opposite ÷ hypotenuse?

Try it

opposite ÷ hypotenuse
adjacent ÷ hypotenuse
opposite ÷ adjacent
Big idea. In a right-angled triangle, the angle decides the shape. The size doesn't change the shape. So each angle has its own fixed ratios, like a fingerprint.

Those three fingerprints were useful enough to get names:

sine of the angle = opposite ÷ hypotenuse

cosine of the angle = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse

tangent of the angle = opposite ÷ adjacent

So when your calculator says sin 35° = 0.574, it's telling you: in every right-angled triangle with a 35° angle, the opposite side is 0.574 of the hypotenuse. Check it against the slider.

Where do the numbers come from?

Before calculators, people wrote tables of these fingerprints by hand. The first known ones were made by Hipparchus, a Greek astronomer, around 140 BC. How would you make one?

Here's a neat move: since size doesn't matter, pick the easiest size. Make the hypotenuse exactly 1. Then sine = opposite ÷ 1 = just the height. And cosine is just the distance across.

Now spin that hypotenuse of length 1 around a point. Its tip draws a circle.

Unroll the circle and you get a wave

Let the dot keep going round. Every moment, write down its height. Plot those heights one after another.

Watch

That shape is the sine wave. Anything that goes round and round, or back and forth, makes one: a Ferris wheel seat, a swing, the tide, a guitar string, the electricity in your wall socket, the sound of your voice.

Try it: the Ferris wheel

A Ferris wheel has radius 10 m and its centre is 12 m above the ground. Your seat starts level with the centre and goes anticlockwise. When the wheel has turned through angle θ, your height is

height = 12 + 10 × sin θ

Why does that make sense? (Centre, plus how far up the circle you are.) How high are you after 30°?

And after 270°?

Room 7 of 9

A better way to measure angles

Why are there 360° in a full turn? No deep reason. The Babylonians liked the number 360 (it splits nicely, and it's close to the days in a year). It's a human choice, like inches.

Here's a choice that comes from the circle itself: measure an angle by how far you walk around the edge of a circle with radius 1. Walk 1 unit of arc and you've turned 1 radian.

Predict first

How far do you walk to go all the way round? (Circumference of a circle = 2πr, and r = 1.)

The reward: tiny angles

For a very small angle, the arc you walked and the height you climbed are almost the same thing (zoom into the circle near the start and the edge is nearly straight up). Tap the grey cells to reveal sin x.

angle x (radians)sin x

For small angles, sin x ≈ x, but only in radians. In degrees you'd get sin 1° ≈ 0.01745, nowhere near 1. Engineers use this every day, and it's why physics uses radians.

Room 8 of 9 · bonus, links to calculus

How steep is a wave?

You've met differentiation: the gradient of a curve at a point. Let's ask it of the sine wave. You don't need any rules for this, just your eyes.

Predict first

Where is the sine wave climbing most steeply?

Slide along the wave

gradient of sin at x
cos x
Big idea. The gradient of sin x is cos x, everywhere. Look back at the circle and it makes sense: when the dot is at the side (cos = 1) it's moving straight up, fastest; when it's at the top (cos = 0) it's moving sideways, not climbing at all.

And this is why mathematicians insist on radians. In degrees the gradient comes out as 0.01745 × cos x, with that ugly number from the last room tagging along. In radians it's simply cos x. The "natural" unit gives the clean answer.
